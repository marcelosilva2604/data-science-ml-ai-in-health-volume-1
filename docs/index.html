Foundations of Data Analysis in Health

Data, Study Design, Probability, and Classical Inference · Volume 1 · companion notebooks

Companion notebooks to the book (they do not reproduce it): Part A follows the chapter's case, Part B gives the code and result of each exercise. All data are synthetic.

  • Chapter 2. Populations, Samples, and Data
    Case: the manager of a 500-bed hospital audits a sample of the January discharges to home to see how often discharge is delayed.
  • Chapter 3. Variables and Measurement Scales
    Case: an attending physician starts the morning shift of an adult emergency department and reads a triage log of 200 visits.
  • Chapter 4. Describing One Variable
    Case: an author describes a cohort of 300 adults admitted with community-acquired pneumonia for the first table of a manuscript.
  • Chapter 5. Measures of Frequency in Health
    Case: the health department of a municipality of 200,000 inhabitants checks the headline numbers of its annual report.
  • Chapter 6. Describing Two Variables
    Case: the medical director of an emergency department studies one month of the triage log: arrival route, triage level, admission, and waiting.
  • Chapter 7. Measures of Association and Impact in Health
    Case: a research fellow checks the draft of an abstract from a register of 2,400 elective-surgery patients: diabetes, smoking, a decolonization bundle, and infection.
  • Chapter 8. Observational Study Design in Health
    Case: the quality directorate of a network of twelve hospitals asks which study would show that a preoperative bundle lowers surgical-site infection.
  • Chapter 10. Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs in Health
    Case: a hypertension service with ten clinics decides whether to offer a single pill instead of separate tablets.
  • Chapter 11. Probability: The Language of Uncertainty
    Case: the infection-control committee of a public maternity unit reviews a register of 5,000 deliveries.
  • Chapter 13. Sampling and Estimation
    Case: a health department draws a sample of schoolchildren for an oral-health survey, from a list of 14,299 children in 48 schools.

More chapters will be added as they are finished. Each chapter is self-contained: it reads only its own data.
